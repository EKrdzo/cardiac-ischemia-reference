What was measured, and how it tracks coronary ischaemia

Every feature recorded in heart_disease_Final.csv, what it means clinically, and how strongly it separates the patients this file labels as having angiographic disease — “> 50% diameter narrowing” in a major vessel, in the source dictionary's own words — from those it labels as not. That label records a lumen measurement, not confirmed ischaemia, and section 2 says what follows from the difference. All figures are computed live in your browser from the embedded records — nothing is fetched, and nothing is hand-typed.

Read this first. This is a teaching and analysis page for a university course, not a clinical decision tool. The numbers describe a specific historical research cohort — they do not diagnose anyone, and none of these features should be read as an individual risk estimate. 3 · Data quality — read before any other section

The same file hides four different ways of saying “missing”

This is not a clean dataset, and a careless analysis of it fails silently: an ordinary CSV reader accepts all four conventions as real measurements. The toggle below lets you watch exactly how wrong the summary statistics get.

Why this matters before it is interesting

A resting blood pressure of −1000 mmHg is physiologically impossible, but it is a perfectly valid number to a spreadsheet. Left in, it drags the cohort mean resting BP down by roughly 12 mmHg and sets the reported minimum to a negative pressure. A cholesterol of 0 mg/dl is read as a real (absurd) value, pulling the mean from — to — mg/dl.

6 · The headline finding

A missing value that is itself a signal

The most interesting result in this dataset is not one of the clinical features. It is what happens to the diagnosis rate among the patients whose cholesterol was never recorded — and it is the reason the “exclude impossible values” checkbox at the top of this page is a choice rather than a default you can trust.

Diagnosis rate conditioned on each defect

Bars: rate among rows carrying that sentinel. Whiskers: 95% Wilson. Line: the — cohort baseline.

Only one of these survives correction for looking six times

Testing six sentinel probes and then reporting whichever difference looks biggest is how false findings get made. Under a Bonferroni threshold of —, only Cholesterol = 0 survives. The RestingBP −14 pp effect, which looks striking side by side with it, does not: p = — uncorrected, and the combined 52-row RestingBP group reaches p = —. Report it as “not distinguishable from noise”, because that is what it is.

And even the survivor should not be read clinically

An 88.4% disease rate hiding inside absent data is far too strong to be a comfortable finding. Missingness that correlates this tightly with the outcome is the classic signature of an artefact of dataset construction, of an encoding that leaks the label, or of a genuine but unrecorded clinical process (for example lipid panels being omitted precisely because a patient was already acutely ill). This page cannot distinguish those three, so it does not pretend to. What it does assert is narrower and defensible: dropping the 172 cholesterol-zero rows is not a neutral act. It removes 152 of the 508 positive cases — 30% of all positives in the file — and quietly moves the target prevalence from 55.3% to roughly 47%.

      11 · Appendix

      Corrections made during preparation

      The corrections that changed what this page prints. They stay visible because several of the numbers involved circulate in the wider literature, and a reference page is easier to trust when it shows where it was wrong. Identifiers were resolved against the registry that owns them — lookup by PMID or PMCID rather than a title search, which in this subject area answers a 1986 query with 2023–2026 papers. That difference is what caught two entries whose identifiers resolved perfectly well, to other people’s research: PMC4058815, a group key-exchange protocol, and PMC6281597, a study of granulocyte cell death. Folded items open in a printed copy.

      Sex — “women 21 %, men 12 %” had the direction backwards

      The CONFIRM young-cohort sex difference was printed the wrong way round for one round of checking. PMID 25539786 reads “Compared with women, men demonstrated higher rates of any CAD (21 vs. 12 %, P < 0.001)” — men 21 %, women 12 %. The inversion outlived a checker that discarded every percentage below 100 and so had never compared those two figures at all.

      A later draft also credited women with “less typical complaints”, citing a 1,028-patient outpatient study. That paper concludes that women and men “present quite similar if presenting with chest pain”, so the symptom claim is gone. What survives from it is the age difference: women about two years older at evaluation, 58 versus 56 years, P = 0.009.

      Cholesterol — a 45 % / 47 % accuracy pair, withdrawn

      Total cholesterol described as roughly 45 % sensitive and 47 % specific for obstructive coronary disease was credited to the NHANES I Epidemiologic Follow-up Studies. No such analysis reports it, and structurally none could: that cohort ascertained deaths and non-fatal coronary events by interview and record review and never performed angiography, so it holds no reference standard against which to score a test. The figure is withdrawn rather than softened, and Cholesterol is described here without an accuracy claim attached.

      The same pass settled a question two drafts answered differently. One asserted that the 2018 cholesterol report “has since been retired and replaced” without looking anything up; the next asserted that no replacement could be resolved anywhere. A direct identifier lookup found it — PMID 41824552 / 10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423, whose abstract states that it retires and replaces the 2018 document. The guideline cited on this page is the 2026 one.

      FastingBS — the documented threshold is 120 mg/dl, not 160

      A source marked verified for this file’s FastingBS flag carried “fasting blood sugar > 160 mg/dl (1 = true; 0 = false)”. The documentation it cites defines the flag as > 120 mg/dl, and “> 160” occurs nowhere in that document — checked in both the rendered text (8,821 characters) and the payload it ships (146,411 characters). This is the only case where a cited document was read and disagreed, so the quotation went with the identifier. The threshold printed in the dictionary entry is the documented >120 mg/dl. A widely repeated number that does not match its primary source is still the kind of error a reader inherits.

      MaxHR — a standard error credited to the wrong cohort

      Two attributions were wrong on one card. The standard error of estimate of 11.35 beats/min belongs to the 3,796-subject validation cohort of the age-prediction registry, not to that paper’s separate 1,000-subject cross-validation sample; and the 351-study meta-analysis behind 208 − 0.7 × age prints no standard error of any kind, so it may not be credited with either figure. The page quotes the registry’s published Bland–Altman limits (+25.31 / −24.67 beats) instead of doubling a standard error computed here.

      A cohort size of 2,953 was also arithmetic rather than published: it is the sum of that source’s two sex counts. The counts — 1,877 men and 1,076 women — are printed; the sum is not.

      ChestPainType and ST_Slope — two “adds nothing prognostically” claims, withdrawn

      ChestPainType. An earlier draft asserted that chest-pain characteristics carry no independent prognostic value and cited a paper. That work could not be found in any registry, and the retrievable literature says close to the opposite: in 38,383 patients followed for a median of 10.9 years, typical angina was associated with substantially more ischaemia than other clinical symptoms, and the authors argue that early revascularisation of exactly those patients masks the prognostic signal rather than abolishing it (adjusted hazard ratio 0.72, 95 % CI 0.57–0.92). What the card keeps is the narrower point it can support: the order typical → atypical → non-anginal as recorded in this file is a coding order, not a risk gradient.

      ST_Slope. “The ST response adds no prognostic information once the treadmill score is known” was withdrawn for the same reason — the work cited for it could not be found, and the nearest retrievable evidence points the other way — rather than softened into a hedge. That entry now carries only what its two remaining sources cover, and keeps the warning that any ratio between two of those estimates is arithmetic done on this page, not a published contrast.
